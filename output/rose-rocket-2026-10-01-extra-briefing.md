## Abstract

This paper addresses an assignment to analyze the truth-seeking and confirmation methods in Chapters 2 and 7 of Logan Cross’s Conspiracy Theories Unveiled (Rivanna Heights Media, 2024) and to map those methods onto an artificial-intelligence platform for the pursuit and preservation of knowledge, here designated C.A.N.O.N. (Claim Assessment, Notation, Ontology, and Network). Open, legal research confirms that the book is a commercially published title with identifiable ISBNs and publisher footprint. It does not confirm the content of Chapters 2 or 7: no table of contents, chapter excerpts, or secondary methodological summaries were located. Accordingly, this paper refuses fabricated Cross quotations, documents the verification gap in full, and develops a clearly labeled comparable truth-validation design drawn from epistemology, open-source intelligence (OSINT), scientific peer review, and professional fact-checking. That design is then engineered, subsystem by subsystem, into C.A.N.O.N. Real-world analogues and a works-cited apparatus conclude the paper. The central thesis is epistemic: bibliographic existence does not license chapter-level attribution, and an honest AI knowledge platform must encode that same discipline as software.

Keywords: conspiracy literature; verification; epistemology; OSINT; fact-checking; knowledge graphs; AI governance; Logan Cross

## 1. Introduction

Assignments that demand deep analysis of named chapters presuppose access to those chapters. When access fails, two dishonest paths are common: invent the missing prose, or treat marketing blurbs as method. This paper takes a third path. It (1) verifies what can be verified about Cross’s book, (2) states without euphemism what cannot be verified about Chapters 2 and 7, (3) substitutes a labeled comparable confirmation pipeline rather than fictional Cross technique lists, and (4) cross-examines that pipeline as a software architecture—C.A.N.O.N.—built for knowledge pursuit and preservation rather than narrative victory.

The practical stakes are straightforward. A reply that invents “Chapter 2’s five tools” would fail the very standard of confirmation the assignment invokes. A reply that stops at “I couldn’t find the book” would understate the evidence: the book is findable; the chapters are not readable from public previews. Precision about that distinction is the paper’s first product.

## 4. Marketing claims are not a validation method

Without inventing chapter content, the only attributable public claims about Cross’s approach are promotional: interest in truth-seeking and challenging official narratives; discussion of belief psychology and media; assertion of meticulous research, credible sources, and historical context; and inclusion of both contested theories and a set labeled confirmed.

These claims do not specify intake rules, source-ranking criteria, falsification procedures, confidence scoring, or confirmation thresholds. Treating them as Cross’s proved process is circular: marketing asserts quality; the researcher cannot inspect the proof. In C.A.N.O.N. terms, marketing blurbs are tier-low evidence about the author’s self-presentation, not primary evidence of method.

## 5. A comparable truth-validation framework

Because Chapters 2 and 7 are inaccessible, the following framework is not attributed to Logan Cross. It is a composite of fallibilism and falsification, burden of proof, Wikipedia-style source reliability and verifiability, OSINT claim decomposition, CRAAP-style source heuristics, scientific peer review and correction culture, and International Fact-Checking Network (IFCN)–aligned transparency norms. It describes what a rigorous confirmation process looks like when designed for knowledge preservation rather than persuasion.

Step A — Claim intake and scoping. Accept a claim as a string to be tested, not as a conclusion. Record provenance (who, where, when), stakes (impact, virality, risk), and verifiability (whether primary evidence could exist).

Step B — Atomic claim decomposition. Split compound narratives into independently testable propositions. Separate facts, inferences, and value judgments. Surface hidden assumptions. Keep human editability mandatory; language models mis-parse compound claims.

Step C — Source ranking. Prefer independent primary evidence; then high-quality secondary analysis with editorial control and a reputation for accuracy; treat anonymous, viral, or self-published claims as low tier unless corroborated. Reliability is claim-relative: a source strong for one proposition may be weak for another.

Step D — Corroboration and contradiction search. Require independent lines of evidence. Actively seek disconfirming data. Distinguish unverified from false.

Step E — Bias and methodology critique. Examine conflicts of interest, selection bias, sample size, replication, and framing. Evaluate what is claimed and how the claimant reached it as separate questions.

Step F — Confidence scoring and verdict taxonomy. Use graded outcomes (for example true, mostly true, mixed, mostly false, false, unverifiable) with explicit high / medium / low confidence tied to evidence quality and independence.

Step G — Audit trail and revision. Log sources consulted, failed searches, and later corrections. Preserve conclusions as editable when better evidence arrives.

This composite is the honest substitute for “Cross’s method” until Chapters 2 and 7 can be cited with page numbers from a purchased or licensed copy.

## 6. Cross-examination: engineering the framework as C.A.N.O.N.

C.A.N.O.N.—Claim Assessment, Notation, Ontology, and Network—is proposed here as an AI platform for the pursuit and preservation of knowledge. It is neither a conspiracy amplifier nor a pure debunking appliance. Each validation step maps to a software subsystem.

### 6.1 Intake layer

An API or user interface accepts submissions (URL, text claim, document hash). Metadata capture includes optional submitter identity, timestamp, language, and media type. A priority queue scores impact times virality times verifiability times harm-if-wrong. Policy gates reject illegal content for storage or action and quarantine contested living-person claims for human review.

### 6.2 Claim decomposition engine

A natural-language pipeline extracts atomic propositions and entity links (people, organizations, dates, places). Graph nodes include Claim, SubClaim, Assumption, and Inference. Outputs remain human-editable so analysts can correct model errors before scoring.

### 6.3 Source ranking service

A classifier labels sources as primary, secondary, or tertiary; records institutional tier and editorial standards; and features prior accuracy, correction history, independence from the subject, and peer-review status. Circular sourcing and language-model-hallucinated citations are explicitly down-ranked. Wikipedia-like rules apply: no source is always reliable; reliability is claim-relative.

### 6.4 Contradiction detection and evidence graphs

Evidence graphs use nodes for documents or observations and edges for supports, contradicts, neutral, or depends-on. A multi-agent pattern—parallel agents for fact check, methods critique, bias detection, and context—synthesizes only after independent runs, reducing single-pass confirmation bias. Queries surface orphan claims (no independent support) and forked consensus (two coherent but incompatible evidence clusters).

### 6.5 Confidence scoring

A calibrated ensemble combines independent primary count, methodological soundness, bias flags, recency, and replication. The system never collapses to a binary true/false without an unverifiable state and a confidence interval. Explainability publishes why scores move when new evidence arrives.

### 6.6 Audit logs and knowledge retention

An immutable append-only ledger records inputs, model versions, sources fetched, human overrides, and final verdicts. A versioned knowledge base treats supersession as first-class. Export formats such as BibTeX and JSON-LD keep preserved knowledge portable outside the platform.

### 6.7 Human review gates

Mandatory human sign-off covers living-person reputational claims, high-impact public-safety claims, and low-confidence high-virality items. Reviewer interfaces show the evidence graph, not only the model summary, so humans can disagree with the AI. Appeals and correction workflows mirror IFCN-style transparency obligations.

### 6.8 Honest cross-examination under uncertainty

If Cross’s inaccessible chapters later prove to emphasize unstructured “question everything” rhetoric without falsification, source ranking, and auditability, they would be weaker than C.A.N.O.N.’s design. If they emphasize independent corroboration and psychology of belief—as marketing themes hint—those ideas would align with Steps B–E above. Alignment remains inference from blurbs, not demonstration from chapter text. When a licensed copy arrives, the correct research move is to extract Chapters 2 and 7 with page citations and re-run this mapping against Cross’s actual steps.

## 7. Real-world analogues

1. Wikipedia: Reliable sources / Verifiability — Articles rest on published sources with a reputation for fact-checking; reliability is contextual; exceptional claims need exceptional sourcing.
2. Scientific peer review and replication — Pre-publication critique, preference for secondary reviews and meta-analyses, and correction or retraction culture.
3. Professional fact-checkers (for example Snopes; IFCN Code of Principles) — Claim selection, primary-source contact where possible, transparent sourcing, corrections, and nonpartisanship standards.
4. OSINT verification practice — Atomic claim breaking, evidence logs, failed-search documentation, and confidence by component rather than by story.
5. CRAAP source heuristics (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) — Lightweight ranking features implementable as scoring signals.

None of these analogues cite Logan Cross; they fill the verification gap left by inaccessible Chapters 2 and 7.

Conspiracy Theories Unveiled by Logan Cross (Rivanna Heights Media / independently published editions, 2024) is a real commercial book with identifiable ISBNs and publisher footprint. That is the limit of what open research can responsibly affirm about the assignment’s core text.
